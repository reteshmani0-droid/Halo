An assistant that lives on your computer, not on someone else's server.

Halo installs on Windows, macOS or Linux and runs there. It opens your apps, types into them, reads your screen to check its own work, and remembers what you have told it. You talk to it at the desk, by voice, from your phone, or from this site.

What it actually does

It drives the computer

Open an app, focus the right window, type, press keys, click, and take a screenshot when it needs to look. It waits for the window it just opened instead of typing into whatever happened to be in front.

It checks its own work

After a step that changed the screen, it looks at a screenshot and answers whether the task is actually done: done, not done, or could not tell. A wrong answer is cheap; a confident wrong one is not.

It remembers, without a memory tab

Facts it learns are stored as facts, not as a growing transcript, and are recalled when they are relevant. Nothing about them is shown to anybody else.

It learns skills you did not ship

Ask it to do something it cannot do yet and it can write the skill, keep it, and use it next time — with the same approval rules as any other action.

It reaches your phone

A Telegram bot, so you can ask it something from outside the house. Only the chats you pair with it are obeyed.

It answers to you, not to us

There is no account server in the middle of a conversation. This site is the dashboard: which machines exist, what they have been doing, and a switch to cut a machine off.

Where the account comes in

The desktop app works with no account at all. An account is what puts a machine on this site's dashboard: its name, when it was last seen, which model it is set to, and its recent messages, so you can read the conversation from a phone.

It is also the only way to cut access to a machine from somewhere else. A Halo install that has been told it is cut off stops accepting remote access on its next check, and says so on its own screen. Nothing on the web can open a connection into your PC; the PC is the one that asks.

What leaves your machine

Only if you turn it on, and only these things:

  • the machine's name, platform, version and current model
  • the messages you and Halo exchanged, so the dashboard can show them
  • whether remote access is on, and when it was last heard from

Not your files, not your keys, not your screen. The site cannot open a socket on your computer or run anything on it. Turning the account off stops the reporting as well.

Questions

Which build should I download?

ARM64 for any Apple silicon Mac and for Windows on ARM (Snapdragon laptops, Windows Dev Kit, Parallels on a Mac). x64 for an Intel or AMD PC. If you are not sure, the download page marks the build for the system you are reading it on.

What does it need besides the installer?

A model to talk to. Halo speaks to an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, so an API key from any provider that offers one is enough, and the key is stored on your machine. A separate vision model can be configured for the screen checks, or the same one can do both.

Is the account required to use Halo?

No. The app runs signed out, and everything local keeps working. The account adds the dashboard, the message history on the web, and the ability to cut a machine off from this site.

Can someone at this site control my computer?

No. The dashboard can read what the app reports, ask it to cut its remote access off, and nothing else. Approving a destructive step always happens on the machine itself, by whoever is sitting at it.

How do I stop a machine reporting in?

Turn the account off in the app's settings; it stops contacting this site immediately. An admin can also cut a machine off, which the app notices on its next check and acts on by disabling the account there.
